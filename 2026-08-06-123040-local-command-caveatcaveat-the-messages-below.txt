  Top pick: #1 — upstream food surplus, not consumer food waste

  Why this wins the balance. Everyone builds consumer food-waste apps. The
  losses are upstream and the NZ evidence is unusually strong:

  - Lineage + NZ Food Network moved >1,000 pallets / ~635,000 kg of frozen 
  vegetables rejected purely for cosmetic irregularities. That's your killer
  data slide.
  - 1 in 3 NZ households hit food insecurity in the past year (Hunger Monitor
  2025, pub. March 2026). Pacific peoples 64%, Māori 51%, ages 18–24 50%.
  - Household waste is actually falling (12.2% → 10.9%; $3.2B → $3.0B). So
  "consumers waste food" is a weakening story. Upstream isn't.

  The real gap, confirmed by research: NZ food rescue matching is route-based 
  and manual, not a marketplace. NZFN's own stated fix for bulk surplus is "be a
  single point of contact" — i.e. a human broker. Regular donors go on a fixed
  recurring route. No shared matching platform surfaced in any search. You'd be
  building something that genuinely doesn't exist, for 64 hubs and 2,000+
  frontline groups moving ~28M meals/yr.

  The AI that isn't a wrapper. Constraint-solving matcher: shelf-life ×
  cold-chain availability × hub capacity × travel time × need-severity. Add a
  vision model classifying cosmetic-reject grade from a grower's phone photo.
  That's agentic + multimodal — exactly what the research says beats generic LLM
  wrappers.

  Hard constraint to name in the pitch (shows depth): Food Act 2014 excludes
  food with compromised sanitary seals unless heated. Knowing this signals you
  talked to the domain, not just an LLM.

  Runner-up: #2 — EGL reassessment navigator

  Strongest timeliness play, and it hits the accessibility brief without being a
  chatbot.

  The system is changing under people's feet right now: from 1 Apr 2026 existing
  NASC users get a set budget with a limit; 1 Oct 2026 reassessments run under
  the new system. Budgets are based on past spending — which CCS Disability
  Action and Blind Low Vision NZ are both actively contesting. EGL Waikato 
  whānau keep direct funding — your venue is inside the affected region.

  Tool: model my budget under new rules → what changed → what I can still
  purchase → generate my reassessment evidence pack. Concrete, verifiable,
  deeply needed, and nobody in the room will have it.

  stock is already useless — a narrow, addressable slice of the vaccine-access  
  problem. SDG — People (SDG 3, Good Health): Directly protects the potency of  
  health commodities reaching underserved patients.

  events are hash-stamped so a spoiled batch can't be quietly un-flagged.

  One thing you need to know about your idea

  The device you described already exists and is WHO-prequalified. Nexleaf
  ColdTrace X: ±0.5°C, 18,000 data points, BLE 5.0 sensors, 2G/NB-IoT/WiFi base
  station, 60-month battery, backend runs 15,000+ devices. Their Transport app
  went wide April 2026. Berlinger Fridge-tag is the standalone incumbent.

  Pitch "ESP32 logs temperature to a dashboard" and a technical judge who knows
  the space places you behind a 5-year-warranty commercial product.

  But the reframe is strong. Field research says the sensor isn't the gap:
  - 91.7% of facilities studied had no cold chain policy
  - 100% had no equipment contingency plan
  - Alert fires, nobody knows who acts

  So I kept your hardware as the demo prop and moved the product to the response
  layer: excursion → agentic triage producing a per-vaccine dose disposition
  (use/quarantine/discard, with cited reasoning) + escalation routing +
  auto-drafted incident record. That step is manual everywhere today.

  Your hash-stamping instinct survives intact — reads as integrity engineering.

  CCM. Standards are under review right now by Health NZ. And there's a live

  monitoring plus out-of-hours alarming. Freezing, not heat, is NZ's most common
  damage cause. Killer stat: NZ cut vaccine wastage 17% → 2%, ~NZ$4M/yr saved.

  Research changed two things in your framing. Both make the pitch stronger:

  1. It's a legal right, not a service improvement. Right 5 of the Code of
  Health and Disability Services Consumers' Rights guarantees communication "in 
  a form, language, and manner that enables the consumer to understand the 
  information provided" — plus a competent interpreter where practicable. Right
  6 covers being fully informed. A consent form the patient didn't understand is
  arguably non-compliant. That's a much harder hook than "patients find this
  confusing."

  2. Drop "compliance." NZ research consistently frames post-discharge failure
  as system-side — inadequate support, medication problems, unpreparedness for
  community living, limited whānau support, poor access to culturally responsive
  services. Readmission signals unmet need, not patient fault. Saying "poor
  patient compliance" in front of NZ health judges would cost you.

  On novelty: Epic's patient-facing assistant Emmie already answers patient
  questions in MyChart and produces plain-language summaries of lab and
  procedure results. But two gaps are real — Epic has no shipped feature
  rewriting the full inpatient discharge summary, and NZ public hospitals don't
  run Epic, so none of it reaches a patient at Waikato Hospital.

  Evidence is strong: LLM-transformed discharge summaries moved 11th-grade →
  6th-grade reading level, understandability 13% → 81%. Same study introduced
  some errors, which is why I put a clinician approval gate in the middle of
  your approach rather than shipping straight to the patient. Demoing that gate
  catching an error is more convincing than claiming the model is always right.

  One positioning warning I built into the doc: health chatbots are the most
  crowded category at SDG hackathons. Your Q&A box is the smallest part of the
  build and should be the smallest part of the pitch. Lead with the consent form
  and Right 5.

  Numbers now in the doc: 56% of NZ adults (1.6M+) can't understand basic health
  information; ~90% of Pacific people 15+ have low health literacy; 16.1% of
  over-65 acute admissions readmitted within 30 days, higher for Māori and
  Pacific.

  Rated it lowest 48h risk of all six — PDF extraction, one extraction call, one
  rewrite call, a review screen. No training, no hospital integration. Good fit
  if your team is stronger on presentation than engineering.
